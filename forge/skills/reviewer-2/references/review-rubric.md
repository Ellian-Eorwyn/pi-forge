# Review Rubric

What deserves a comment, how hard to push, and how to be critical about a social
science or humanities argument without sliding into either flattery or taste.
The mechanical side — field names, callout syntax, what the script refuses — is
`comment-format.md`.

The standard to hold: a reviewer whose report the author is glad they got.
Reviewer 2's criticality is worth keeping. Reviewer 2's uselessness is the part
being replaced, and it has a specific cause — the objection arrives without the
remedy, so the author is told they are wrong and left to guess what right looks
like.

## Read for the argument first

Before writing anything, reconstruct in your own words: what is the article's
central claim, what would have to be true for it to hold, and what does it offer
as reason to believe it. A review written while reading top to bottom finds
sentence-level problems. A review written after that reconstruction finds the
problem that makes half the sentence-level ones symptoms.

Most reviews that read as unhelpful are unhelpful because the reviewer never did
this. They argue with paragraph 14 without noticing that paragraph 4 already
conceded the point.

## What deserves a comment

**Research gaps** (`gap`). A literature the article needed and did not use; a
prior finding that complicates the claim; a case, period, or population whose
absence changes what the argument can conclude. The useful version names the
work and says what engaging it would do to the argument — confirm it, bound it,
or force a revision. "The literature review is thin" is not a comment.

**Thin evidence** (`evidence`). A claim carrying more weight than its support.
The forms this takes in these disciplines: an empirical generalization resting
on one illustrative case; a quantitative word with no number behind it; an
interview count that never becomes an argument about saturation or range; an
archival claim from a single document; a citation doing work its source cannot
do. Say what evidence would be sufficient, not merely that this is not.

**Logic** (`logic`). The inference does not go through. Watch for: a mechanism
asserted rather than demonstrated; correlation narrated as cause; a conclusion
broader than the evidence base; a rival explanation the article never rules out;
a definition that shifts between sections; a claim that is unfalsifiable as
stated. Name the step that fails, not the conclusion you dislike.

**Theoretical engagement** (`theory`). This is where a review earns its keep in
these fields, and where a shallow reviewer stops. The article uses a concept
without its tradition; reinvents a named mechanism under a new label; borrows a
framework while ignoring the critiques that reshaped it; or treats a contested
term as settled. The comment should say which tradition, what it would give the
argument, and what it would cost — a framework is not free, and telling an
author to add Bourdieu without saying what changes is not engagement either.

**Structure** (`structure`). The argument is there but the article does not
deliver it: the thesis arrives on page nine; methods do work the findings never
use; a section answers a question nobody asked; the conclusion introduces a new
claim. These often need no suggested text, because the fix is a move rather
than a sentence.

**Strengths** (`strength`). Use sparingly and only for something real — an
analytic move that works, a piece of evidence carrying genuine weight, a
distinction worth keeping. Two purposes: the author needs to know what not to
cut in revision, and a review with nothing good in it gets read defensively.
Never manufacture one for balance.

## What does not deserve a comment

- Grammar, spelling, word choice, sentence rhythm. Not a close second, not "in
  passing." The script does not stop you and the thinking model will flag it.
- Preferring your own vocabulary for the author's concept.
- The article the author did not write. A review of a different project is the
  archetypal Reviewer 2 failure.
- Citations to your own priors that would not change the argument.
- Severity inflation. If everything is `major`, nothing is.

If the substance is sound, say so and stop. "This article is in good shape;
here are three small things" is a legitimate review and a rare, valuable one.

## Discipline differs; the standard is internal

Evidence norms are not portable, and applying one field's to another's article
is a way to be confidently wrong. Judge the article against the standard of the
tradition it is working in:

- **Quantitative work** — sampling, identification, measurement validity, what
  the model can and cannot license.
- **Qualitative and ethnographic work** — range and saturation, positionality
  where it bears on access, whether the analytic claim exceeds what the
  fieldwork can show. Asking an ethnography for a larger n usually misreads
  what it is for; asking it what a negative case would look like does not.
- **Historical and archival work** — provenance, whose records survived,
  whether absence of evidence is being read as evidence of absence.
- **Interpretive and textual work** — whether the reading is supported by the
  text, whether rival readings are engaged, whether the frame is imported or
  earned.
- **Philosophical and normative work** — validity of the argument, strength of
  the premises, the best objection rather than the easiest one. Empirical
  under-citation is often not the failure here; an unaddressed counterexample
  is.

When a criticism only holds under a different tradition's norms, either drop it
or make the disagreement explicit and arguable rather than smuggling it in as a
defect.

## Writing the fix

Every non-strength comment names its fix. Where the fix is a sentence or a
paragraph, write it — that is the promise of this skill — and write it in the
article's own register, not in review-ese. The author should be able to paste it
and keep reading.

Where the fix is a move — cut a section, relocate the thesis, restrict the
claim, run an analysis — say exactly what the move is and skip the suggested
text. A structural comment padded with invented prose is worse than one without.

Never suggest text that asserts something you have not checked. If the point
needs a source, run `web-research academic` for it. If the source's full text
was never retrieved, hedge and say so — the script enforces the words, but the
honesty is the point, not the string.

## Tone

Criticize the argument. The author is not in the room and cannot answer, which
is exactly why the register matters. Concretely:

- "The mechanism is asserted rather than shown" — not "the author does not
  understand what a mechanism is."
- "This reading needs the demarcation literature to be arguable" — not "this is
  naive."
- Name what would change your mind. A criticism that no evidence could answer is
  a preference wearing a lab coat.
- Address the writing, not the writer: no "you appear to be unaware."

Hard is fine. Contemptuous is not, and it is also less useful — an author who
feels attacked argues with the tone and never gets to the substance.

## Calibration

For a full article, roughly eight to twenty comments. Fewer than five on a real
draft usually means the reconstruction step was skipped; more than twenty-five
means sentence-level noise is being logged as findings. Two to five `major`
comments is a normal distribution for a draft worth reviewing at all.

The meta review is what the author reads first and works from. Rank the
weaknesses by how much of the article they compromise, not by where they appear.
Order the fix plan by dependency: the revision that changes what the other fixes
should say comes first.
